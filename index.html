Isolation and Self-Identity

I have “solo travelled” for extended periods twice in my life. The first time, to Italy and then the Balkans, my experience felt liberating and soulful. The second, which included Southeast Asia, Bulgaria, and Portugal, felt barren and shallow, reminiscent of Agnes Vardas Vagabond. It felt dull despite my shifting surroundings. I’ve wondered what caused this distinction in my internal experience - when my external program, the types of sites I visited, and experiences I had seemed to be similar. And I don’t believe it was the destinations, both trips covered a variety of cultures, and if we’re being honest, tourist travel, even in “solo travel style” is much more a reflection of the person than of the place.

So where did this dullness come from? It was clearly not a lack of stimulation. Take meditation, which is distinct from boredom, and perhaps the ultimate absence of stimulation, which happens to pair with liberty and tranquility. Instead, my experience reminded me of the state I achieve in stretches of isolation. It was a state without creative desire, without the sense of presence that joy streams through. It was a state that would allow me to infinitely drone along without catching an unfamiliar train of thought, stuck in a repeating pattern.

In isolation, my thoughts become self-integral. I try to think and without context, I draw a blank. I find I have no way to realize a thought or process or reflect. I try to grasp at floating ideas but it feels like finding something far out in the ocean while drowning in the water. I become detached, amorphous. I can’t even self-reflect, trying to map thought patterns from within the pattern, a meta-reflection that would be me trying to define a logical pattern from within the logical framework itself (Godel’s incompleteness?).

And this stillness with the self feels like a loss of self. Because who am I when disconnected from a reference point? To myself, I am just me. I am home, I am the vessel through which I experience the world, but I am not concretely defined. I am a chassis for the flow of experience rather than anything closed, contained. My material identity, my fashion, my art, my tastes are all defined with relation to my surroundings.

But I’m quite good at masking this itch, this little hint of discomfort. I can grab my phone or eat some food. I watch some TV. Stimulation. Because of the accessibility of these diversions, they are completely separated from intention. There is no string from the self to this activity, no information or self-reflection or identity rooted through the choice to partake. It’s a subhuman distraction, a temporary life supply to keep my physical realization occupied and connected to reality, yet completely detached from my sense of self. It’s worse even than a primal instinct, which reminds you of your physicality.

Without interactivity, without feedback, my thoughts remain uncalibrated, drifting in some direction that is familiar and repetitive, within my patterns, my latent space. So when interacting with people, books, objects that in some way require my attention and are somehow connected to my identity, this flow of consciousness experiences turbulence. Rocks in the water, that block the current, that send pressures and excite the molecules and create variation in the flow. My consciousness is redirected along vectors that wouldn’t generally be part of the natural plane that my thoughts swim along. My natural ideas are challenged, coerced into unfamiliar spaces and directions, played with, kicked around, reflected by overhearing a conversation that I wouldn’t naturally be engaged in and through attention acknowledging this lack of interest, this orthogonality to my identity, and in doing so learning, engaging with my identity still.

When I lose this attention, I begin to take my thoughts for granted. But every thought is a particle, a sample from my distribution of thought. And when this thought is sampled in a conversation, it experiences some sort of feedback. And the actual content of the feedback itself doesn’t matter all that much, because the content belongs to whoever I’m interacting with. But this feedback generates friction for your thought, and the thought must redirect slightly, and this internal reaction is your way to recognize these samples of your overall distribution of thoughts. This is my only way to map out my thought map, piece by piece. This is how I learn about myself, how I get glimpses of my true self. This process, this deep attentiveness in daily life reveals Carl Jung’s Shadow. It turns mundanity into an active process of individuation.

Any judgement you cast is a reflection, an opportunity to see your thoughts. We look through these warped mirrors and piece together our identity, understand ourselves, and see ourselves.

But I can’t say all this without acknowledging that these glimpses can easily remain undigested. Without sitting with yourself, gathering the shards and sifting through them it is impossible to create this map. I become a collection of fragments that don’t align in any particular way. I find that in hectic periods I stop living with intention, I forget that every moment alive is the opportunity to exercise the wonderful right of being myself. I stop noticing little things, and lose my taste. My identity becomes so cluttered and overfilled with patterns that haven’t been digested, or compressed into a form that makes sense for my mind that I become a blank human. Every color blends together and becomes a white slate living on autopilot.

And maybe this is a completely valid state to live in. The state I had unknowingly entered the second time I travelled, perhaps in having too many expectations, too set of a prior that distracted my attention. Surrounded yet completely unattached to the turbulences in the plane of existence, oscillations undigested superpositioning and blending and moving. There is something so fascinating about this type of “go go go” escapism because it’s an escape of life with life - is it really an escape? Am I falling for over self-analyzation? Perhaps the digestion itself is an escape?

When I go through periods of days, or weeks without spending time alone, it feels as if I haven’t seen an old friend in a long time. And then when I see her, she’s completely different. Unknown to me, and neglected in our friendship. Her needs haven’t been heard, and I realize I don’t know the first thing about her, who she is, what she desires. How have the things she’s seen and experienced made her feel? What did she like? What did she dislike? And I can’t answer any of these questions. It is the ultimate abandonment of self-identity, and I find it leads to fear, to insecurity, and to dissatisfaction. Attention dissolves this. Attention creates a home in oneself, a basis for external interaction, and security in something independent of all external stimuli.

What is love made of?

I’ve been thinking about the material properties of love. As in, how can we best characterize love in the way we characterize objects in our world?

Is it a rigid substance? If so, are familial love and romantic and platonic love all fundamentally made of the same substance, with different lived experiences giving it different compositions, different coatings and paints? Or is love more abstract, like a tube that allows different liquids to flow through it? Perhaps love is the facilitation, the space to create these exchanges rather than a thing.

Maybe then love is an opening in something rather than the object itself.

But then that implies there is some barrier that requires breaking down when building love, an enamel that when chiseled establishes the feeling of having fallen in love. Further, if love is destructive rather than constructive, it would imply all types of love are fundamentally the same.

When I think about how I have experienced familial love, versus platonic love, is the fundamental emotion the same? The fundamental grounding for my understanding of love does seem to be shared experience - the ability to understand a human must come from a sense of alignment in some form, either through lived or communicated experience. There exists an idea in language theory - some set of mutual exchanges to arrive at something called a “fixed point” where two individuals can both assert that they mean the same thing. For example, two individuals from different countries and different languages might want to make sure they mean the same thing when one says the word “banana” and one says “banan”. This can happen through some set of actions where the terminal exchange and therefore understanding is reached when both individuals have convincingly demonstrated to each other that the other one indeed means what they mean. For example, one picks up a banana, one says banan, and the other banana. The exchange terminates with mutual agreement. The same identification could have been achieved if the individuals had together discovered a banana for the first time together, in addition to the shared connection over their full (live) demonstration of each other’s discovery.

Shared experience creates the opening for mutually understood communication. Is this true for compatibility or alignment? If someone can fully and effectively communicate their perspective surely this entails understanding. So does a sufficient amount of shared experience necessitate love?

I will perhaps never share the same views or perspectives as my family, on most things - my parents grew up in the Soviet Union and my brother in Russia. Their experiences of life are painted so fundamentally differently than mine. Structurally, our life experiences in every realm - but perhaps familial - have coated our general perspectives to be very different. I do not share their perspectives on a surprising number of topics, but I understand why their perspectives are the way they are. So perhaps the feeling itself does come from an understanding of their experience, or at least an openness to it, rather than complete overlap. How does this look?

The love I feel for my family feels deeply weighed, weighed with years of baggage and shared experience and injected with pain and judgement. It feels heavy, and intimate in a very different way than platonic love. But the fundamental feeling of familiarity, the feeling that falls beneath all of the noise and weight - that might be the same for platonic and familial. So do you grow in love with your family? Perhaps this is why parents immediately feel love for their children, there is a complete map of shared experience from birth. The child is born and grows with the parents. And as the child enters the world and begins to experience stimuli outside of parental boundaries, there is divergence, the terrible twos, teen rebellion.

Maybe we have different substrates flowing through the opening created by our familial love and romantic love, which makes our experience of love so different. But the fundamental construction of these experiences is rooted in the same opening, jarred, hammered, and carved open through different ways for each relationship.

Actualization = becoming full rank. Being able to understand and feel everything in my body. A non-blocking filter for our perceived existence. Unity with this world.

My subspace is the latent space that my thoughts take form in. Inside it, my column space is experiences injested into thoughts and ideas by me. My nullspace is the rest - experiences and perceptions that are unfathomable to me, which perhaps manifests in not caring.

The collective perceived world is still only understood by me through a transformation. However, my actions take reality beyond my projection (unseen to me) in it. Its rowspace is the responses I present and actualize from my thoughts - my expression. Its perceived nullspace is ideas and thoughts unable to manifest in this world. Perhaps this feels like dissipation, leading to frustration, anger, a feeling of blocking.

Maybe being “alive” is the very capacity to transform life. The ability to act as a filter, a transformer of motion through time - experience.

Constantly blocking the flow, with distractions, a lack of attention, builds rigidity. It constrains the filter and allows less life to flow. Maybe this is what it means to feel helpless: you desire to move with flow, naturally, but internal opposing structures - which almost always seem external - prevent this.

Flexibility is the highest value. Being responsive rather than resistant allows more flow in the direction of life, rather than orthogonal to it, rather than dissipation into entropy, like the force of friction.

Allowing oneself to be excited but also steady, when life calls for it.

Focus and attention give flexibility. Being receptive to inputs prevents buildup of fear and inattention, which atrophies ability to understand. Being expressive perpetuates connection.

Two types of frustration? Lack of understanding, and lack of expression. Both constrict the funnel.

Familial sadness

There’s something deeply devastating in the love I feel for my family.

I see myself so much in my parents, in my brother. So much of their character is embedded in me, and mine in them. It’s a type of understanding that I will never share with anyone else.

I talk with my brother about how we see our father’s character in us. I begin to say something and I am washed with a feeling similar to deja vu, a realization that the very source of the thought is seeded in my parents.

And this taints my love. It doesn’t make it weaker. It strengthens it in a deeply painful way. I feel a constant heartbreak for my family, because almost all of the pain I have ever felt also belongs to them. Has also been felt by them.

The very human, very common understanding that life is just difficult, and filled with pain. I know every single human feels it, but with my family, I can replay almost the exact pain they have felt. I know my most awful habits, and sources of shame, and lowest moments, come close - touch the moments they have felt. Maybe not directly, but in similar points of their journey, in similar situations, they must have felt almost like I do.

And why then do I feel so much sadness for them, when I appreciate every sad moment for myself. When the beauty of life that washes over me in sadness is also in them. Why do I pity them, but not myself? Is it some sort of superiority that stems from the magnitude of my understanding and empathy for them? Some sort of false confidence that I bear?

I don’t understand. Perhaps this love is just funneled, is just so vividly emotional that the feeling itself is overwhelming, and unidentifiable.

Perhaps I am mistaken to color it with a negative emotion. Perhaps I should color it grey, or white, a mixture of every emotion I have felt. Or perhaps it is a reflection of my current state. Of fear, and uncertainty, projected directly onto those I feel that I understand them most.

Utopia

I recently watched a Zena Hitz interview where she brought up the tension between telic and atelic life, and it made me start thinking about the telic components of my own life, and what maximizing my intrinsic for-pleasure life would look like. It made me think about what my “utopia” looks like? What is my perfect society?

I built a picture where the society is something like a commune - close friends, family connections, shared goals of living healthily and maintaining the state of the space. But I also certainly wouldn’t remove romance or pain, I wouldn’t remove love and emotions and all these things that Atwood and Huxley have painted as fundamental to a satisfying (not happy) society. Pain, heartbreak, come paired with joyful things. There would be boys and girls that break my heart, there would be grief over my parents dying.

But why is that different from what I’m living now? I go to work. I work on a team for a shared goal, and perhaps the goal isn’t survival, but it is a shared forward progression. I date. I have close friends and family.

I experience highs and lows, and in fact my highs are in proportion to my lows. When I kiteboard, I often feel as if I am experiencing the most sensational experience possible, similarly with concerts - loud music and laser lights completely swallowing me, or visiting a new place, completely new sensations to taste and live there. With this came paired a dullness - whoever manufactured that plane, gear, concert set - it was likely just as boring and draining in the cumulative labor of generating that experience as it was exciting for me. Technology seems to facilitate proportionally thrilling activities to the labor and mundanity put into making that technology accessible.

But why does it have to be? We might draw the metaphor of a hike, a hard, arduous climb up with the gain of the view at the top - proportional in the line of sight as the vertical length of the height as it happens. But I very much enjoy the entire way through a hike - I can’t even say the summit view is my favorite part. And yet if I was taking this journey knowing I wouldn’t see the top, maybe, for example, someone else would get to see the top instead of me, it would make the experience much less pleasurable (perhaps for both of us). Why?

But my broader point is there would still be oscillation within a Utopia. Not because we can’t conceive of a life without it - we have the primary example of this “flatline” life within Buddhism. There would be little highs, and there would be little lows. Why is the magnitude of these experiences important, if they integrate to the same overall experience quality - which I argue is neutrality. Something about simplicity…

Temperature

I am inspired by Aaron Vartiainen’s characterization of people as a temperature gradient. He established a scale of people, from cold to cool to warm to hot, and argued that there is a non-injective connotation to the function of a person’s temperature, where both cool and warm are positive traits, and neutrality and cold are negative traits.

He then poses that individuals can be characterized by multiple temperatures, breaking the functional analysis of this gradient. I pose that these terms are actually orthogonal properties of a person’s character, and our semantic characterization of a person’s warmth and coldness are explained by the dominance of two orthogonal intrinsic properties - rootedness and generalizability.

I think I can explain this using a model of consumption-creation in our interaction with the world. Where each activity an individual performs allows that person to internalize some information, and externalize some information (potentially through physical change). In the following, I am interested in characterizing people based on this dual interaction.

Generalists are flexible consumers and creators. They are people who are good at listening, good at understanding. Perhaps they are incredibly welcoming, or accommodating and able to meet others where they stand, both metaphorically and physically. They are able to be receptive to their environment and adjust their interaction based on it, a feeling we attribute to “warmth”.

In the spatial model, it would be people with a well-defined/high rank world-to-latent mapping. They are sensitive to changes in their environment, and can meet people where they are at.

As an aside - I insert none of the negative connotation of the critique about overconsumption here. I don’t see any reason to believe consumption is less important than creation, and overcreation equally as ill as overconsumption: over-doing anything simply entails time spent living carelessly.
